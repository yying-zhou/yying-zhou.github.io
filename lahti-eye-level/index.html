Open-data study · Lahti, Finland

What a city knows about its pocket parks, and what it can't see

Lahti maps every lawn, shrub group and tree it maintains. It also collects thousands of resident complaints and suggestions. Nothing connects the two. This page shows both datasets and proposes a 3D participatory method to bridge them.

Yingying Zhou · 2026 · data & code · annotation instrument

Small parks are the green space most people actually pass every day. They are also where design details matter most: a dense hedge can shelter birds and block the view of an entrance, and an unmown meadow can read as biodiversity to one resident and as neglect to another. These are the questions a city has to answer park by park, and the data it would need is mostly already there.

All figures below come from two public services of the City of Lahti, downloaded in October 2026: the map service, whose green-area register is published at component level, and the resident feedback service. Nothing here needed a login.

1The city's register is remarkably fine-grained

Lahti does not record a park as a single green blob. It records each lawn, meadow, shrub group, perennial bed and gravel path as a separate polygon, tagged with a maintenance class that tells the contractor how often to mow, prune or clear it. Each tree and shrub is also a point with its species. Of the city's named parks, most are small:

3What residents tell the city

Lahti publishes resident feedback with a category, a short excerpt and the city's handling status. Many items are pinned to a map. Most green-space feedback is about maintenance. Keyword-matching the excerpts (in Finnish) shows what it is about:

Items can match more than one theme. Excerpts are about 150 characters long, so these counts are lower bounds. Only counts are published here, never the texts or locations of individual items.

The gap. A typical complaint might say the bushes by the path are so overgrown it feels unsafe in the evening. That sentence names a feeling and a rough place. The maintenance contractor works from a specific shrub-group polygon with its own register ID and maintenance class. Someone has to translate one into the other by hand, and often nobody does. The translation also only ever runs one way: residents are rarely asked about the shrub group before it is cut back.

4A bridge: annotate at eye level, link to the asset

The proposal is to let residents mark what they value or avoid directly in a photo-real 3D reconstruction of their own park, made with 3D Gaussian Splatting from ordinary phone photos. Each mark is then linked automatically to the city's own register:

1 · Capture

Phone photos → 3D scene

About 30 minutes on site. The scene is aligned to the city's survey control points, so it shares the register's coordinates.

2 · Annotate

Residents mark values at eye level

Recreation, aesthetics, wildlife habitat or avoidance, plus a short note.

3 · Link

Each mark snaps to an asset

e.g. avoidance → shrub group #… · class R3 or habitat → landscape meadow #….

4 · Act

An "asset value layer" for maintenance

Value and avoidance counts per polygon, tree and bench, loaded directly into the city's GIS.

Two things become possible that neither dataset allows alone. First, design changes can be tested before they are made. The same scene can be edited to show a lawn turned into meadow, or a shrub group thinned at an entrance, so residents respond before any work is ordered. Second, the method can be validated. If 3D annotations are more informative than 2D ones, they should predict the independent feedback record and observed park use better.

What this is and is not

  • Feedback is a signal of concern, not of prevalence. People who send feedback are not a representative sample. A park with no feedback may simply have quiet neighbours.
  • "Within 50 m" is an approximation. Points are counted inside a 50 m buffer around each park outline. Busy streets next to a park pull in feedback about the street.
  • Themes are keyword matches on short excerpts. They are indicative only. A real analysis would read the full texts and the city's replies.
  • Homes within 300 m are dwellings in the building register within 300 m of the park outline, a rough catchment in the spirit of the 3‑30‑300 green-space rule.
  • The register shows what the city maintains, not how a place feels. That is the point of the proposal.

Data and code

  • Lahden karttapalvelu, City of Lahti map service. Layers used: green areas (Viheralueet), vegetation (Kasvillisuus), bins and benches, buildings, districts.
  • Lahden palautepalvelu, published resident feedback (list-level fields only).
  • Basemap © OpenStreetMap contributors.

Every step is scripted in Python (requests, pyproj, shapely): download the map layers, download the published feedback, then build the small files this page loads. Coordinates are converted from ETRS-GK26 (EPSG:3880) to WGS84.
